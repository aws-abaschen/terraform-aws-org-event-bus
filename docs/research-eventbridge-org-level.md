# One shared bus reshapes organization-wide EventBridge

AWS guidance now points to a single platform-owned bus for the whole organization: the new Custom Event Bus launched on 24 September 2026 is shared through AWS RAM, and each team owns its own subscribers in its own account, replacing the bus-to-bus forwarding topologies that every earlier AWS source describes ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-enhanced-custom-event-buses-in-amazon-eventbridge-for-enterprise-scale-event-driven-applications/), [AWS Compute Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/compute/building-event-driven-applications-at-scale-with-amazon-eventbridge/)). For a Terraform design with a central-bus module and a spoke-connection module, the new bus is the right target because it is the only EventBridge product whose IAM model exposes arbitrary publisher-supplied attributes (`events:Metadata/<key>`) on both the publish and the subscribe side, which is what a configurable Platform -> Solution -> Product -> Service hierarchy needs ([Custom Event Bus actions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-access-actions.html)). Three caveats shape the design. First, the central ABAC mechanism, comparing an event attribute to `${aws:PrincipalTag/...}`, is permitted by IAM's language rules but appears in no AWS example for EventBridge, so it must be proven in a test before anything depends on it. Second, ABAC covers "publish only what you own" cleanly, but an owner service allow-listing other services is an entitlement list, not an attribute match, and has to be rendered as explicit policy statements inside a 20 KB policy budget. Third, the `hashicorp/aws` provider had no resource for the new bus as of version 6.73.0, so the modules must use CloudFormation-backed resources for now. No dedicated AWS whitepaper on multi-account EventBridge exists; the guidance is spread across the User Guide, blog posts, one Prescriptive Guidance pattern pair and a 2020 re:Invent deck. Every published large-scale deployment also wraps the bus in a platform layer for schemas, validation and subscriptions, which the Terraform modules should be designed to become.

## AWS guidance moved from forwarding meshes to one RAM-shared bus

AWS's multi-account EventBridge guidance comes in three generations, and a design written today should treat only the last as the default. The first (2020 to 2024) is bus-to-bus forwarding under resource policies. The re:Invent 2020 session API302 coined the vocabulary still in use: "single-bus, multi-account" (centralized, a DevOps or platform team owns one bus and its rules) and "multi-bus, multi-account" (distributed, each service team owns its bus, at the price of every producer knowing its consumers) ([re:Invent 2020 API302](https://d1.awsstatic.com/events/reinvent/2020/Building_eventdriven_architectures_API302.pdf), [aws-samples patterns](https://github.com/aws-samples/amazon-eventbridge-resource-policy-samples/blob/main/patterns/README.md)). The Prescriptive Guidance pattern for cross-account connections in an organization belongs to this generation: a bus policy with `Principal: "*"` and an `aws:PrincipalOrgID` condition, one forwarding rule per destination account, and a suggestion to tighten the policy to specific source accounts ([AWS Prescriptive Guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/patterns/create-cross-account-amazon-eventbridge-connection-organization.html)). Its claim that EventBridge can only target a bus in another account is outdated.

The second generation arrived on 21 January 2025, when rules gained direct cross-account delivery to SQS, SNS, Lambda, Kinesis and API Gateway in the same Region, removing the receiving bus for many flows ([AWS Compute Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/compute/introducing-cross-account-targets-for-amazon-eventbridge-event-buses), [AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/01/amazon-eventbridge-direct-delivery-cross-account-targets)). The rule and its execution role must live in the bus-owning account, and `PutTargets` from any other account is unsupported for non-bus targets, so this model keeps routing centrally owned ([EventBridge User Guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-service-cross-account.html)).

The third generation is the September 2026 relaunch. The existing product is now "Custom Event Bus - Classic"; the new "Custom Event Bus" (ARN type `event-busv2`, endpoint `eventsv2`) is positioned as "a single, centralized event bus shared across all AWS accounts in your organization" ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-enhanced-custom-event-buses-in-amazon-eventbridge-for-enterprise-scale-event-driven-applications/), [Names and endpoints](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-names.html)). AWS's own critique of the older model is blunt: "A year later the organization runs dozens of Custom event buses joined by forwarding rules," with cost following topology rather than consumer count ([AWS Compute Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/compute/building-event-driven-applications-at-scale-with-amazon-eventbridge/)). Classic buses keep working unchanged and the new bus is adopted "at your own pace" ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-enhanced-custom-event-buses-in-amazon-eventbridge-for-enterprise-scale-event-driven-applications/)).

Two Classic constraints explain why the old topologies scale badly and should stay out of a new design. Forwarding is **one hop only**: an event forwarded by a rule to a second account is not sent on to a third ([EventBridge User Guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-cross-account.html)). And a central Classic bus carries a default of **300 rules per bus with a hard limit of 5 targets per rule**, which one-rule-per-consumer designs exhaust quickly ([EventBridge quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-quota.html)).

The new bus does not remove every legacy pattern. CloudTrail-delivered events land only on each account's default bus, so centralizing API activity still needs a forwarding rule stamped into every account ([EventBridge User Guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-service-event-cloudtrail.html)). The bus and its consumers must share a Region and partition, so a multi-Region estate needs one bus per Region and a forwarding design between them ([sharing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-sharing.html)). The launch covered 14 Regions (N. Virginia, Ohio, Oregon, Ireland, Frankfurt, Stockholm, Spain, Hong Kong, Malaysia, Mumbai, Singapore, Sydney, Thailand, Tokyo), and the default quota of **5 new-style buses per account** signals that AWS intends few large shared buses, not a bus per domain ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-enhanced-custom-event-buses-in-amazon-eventbridge-for-enterprise-scale-event-driven-applications/), [quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-quota.html)).

## Hierarchy ABAC is expressible on the new bus but unproven

### What each bus lets IAM see

Events carry no tags, so IAM can only reason about the attributes EventBridge projects into the request context. On Classic that is `source` and `detail-type` at publish, plus three fixed AWS-specific `detail.*` keys at rule creation; nothing a producer puts in `detail` is visible ([conditions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-use-conditions.html), [service reference](https://servicereference.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/v1/events/events.json)). A four-level hierarchy on Classic therefore has to be squeezed into one dotted `source` string and enforced by prefix, a use of `StringLike` on `events:source` that AWS never demonstrates.

The new bus changes this. Publishers calling `PutRawEvents` attach metadata keys, and each key becomes a condition key: on publish it holds the event's value, and on `CreateSubscriber` and `UpdateSubscriber` it holds every value the subscriber's `METADATA` filter matches ([actions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-access-actions.html)). A configurable hierarchy maps onto one metadata key per level. AWS publishes a worked example restricting a consumer account to subscribers filtering on `tenant` = `acme`, using three conditions together: a `Null` check requiring the key, `ForAllValues:StringEquals` on the value, and `events:Metadata/tenant/Matcher` = `exact` ([actions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-access-actions.html)). Metadata filters are exact-match only, so "everything under Solution X" is a filter on the `solution` key alone, not a prefix. Two details matter for module design: metadata exists only on `PutRawEvents` (plain `PutEvents` supplies `events:source` and `events:detail-type` instead), and AWS advises separate subscribers for each API shape because a filter written for the wrong shape silently matches nothing ([event structure](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-addressing.html)).

### Documented behavior versus what needs a test

The decisive question is whether a policy can compare those keys with the caller's tags. EventBridge states that policy variables are usable in its conditions, but its only example is `events:creatorAccount` = `${aws:PrincipalAccount}` ([conditions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-use-conditions.html)). IAM's rules support the pattern: `aws:PrincipalTag` is single-valued and so usable as a variable, variables work with string operators in resource policies, and IAM shows several tag variables composed into one string ([IAM policy variables](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_variables.html), [global condition keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_condition-keys.html)). No AWS document, blog or sample shows `events:source` or `events:Metadata/*` compared against a principal tag, and none shows `aws:PrincipalTag` or `aws:PrincipalOrgPaths` in a bus policy at all.

Several documented behaviors constrain the policy shapes the modules generate. Publishing is authorized per entry with no partial authorization, so one bad entry denies the whole batch. Conditions on subscriber filters are checked only at create and at an update that carries a filter configuration; tightening a policy never touches existing subscribers, and unsharing does not stop them either, which makes `RevokeResource` the only real off-switch. A grant on `CreateSubscriber` alone leaves `UpdateSubscriber` unconstrained, so both actions need identical conditions. RAM managed permissions carry no conditions at all ([actions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-access-actions.html), [sharing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-sharing.html)).

### Owner allow-lists are entitlements, not attribute matches

Tag-variable ABAC answers "may this principal publish as service S?" with a constant-size statement, because the principal's own tags describe what it owns. It does not answer "may consumer C read service S?", because C's tags describe C, not the producers it is entitled to read. The research found no AWS source that addresses this; the following is design inference from the documented building blocks. Publish-side control fits a Deny guardrail: deny `PutRawEvents` when any hierarchy metadata key does not equal the corresponding principal tag. Inverted string operators match on a missing value, so untagged principals and events lacking the key fail closed ([IAM policy variables](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_variables.html)). Subscribe-side control needs two statement families: one constant-size statement letting a principal subscribe within its own subtree, and one explicit Allow per owner service listing the consumer identities it has approved, conditioned on the `service` metadata key with the `Null` and `Matcher` checks from AWS's example. Because RAM's `SubscribeOnly` permission is unconditional, a default-closed allow-list means granting subscribe rights through the `default` policy or a RAM customer managed permission instead, and reserving RAM managed permissions for publish.

That choice runs into the policy budget. The `default` policy holds 20,480 bytes by default, adjustable to an undocumented ceiling ([quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-quota.html)). At roughly 350 to 500 characters per statement, that is space for about 40 to 55 owner allow-list statements (arithmetic from the notes, not an AWS figure). Past that scale the allow-list has to move out of the bus policy into a control plane that creates subscribers on consumers' behalf, the approach every published large deployment already takes.

### Tags are only as trustworthy as the organization makes them

Principal tags come from the caller's account. A session tag overrides a role tag with the same key, and transitive tags propagate through role chains ([STS session tags](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/id_session-tags.html)). ABAC on a shared bus is therefore sound only when service control policies stop teams from setting ownership tags on roles and sessions; AWS says tags used for authorization must be protected this way ([AWS Security Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/securing-resource-tags-used-for-authorization-using-service-control-policy-in-aws-organizations)). Tag policies standardize spelling and allowed values but do not evaluate untagged resources, so they are hygiene, not access control ([tag policies](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/organizations/latest/userguide/orgs_manage_policies_tag-policies.html)). Resource control policies now cover the `events` prefix and can enforce "organization principals only" on every bus, with two exceptions: they do not apply to the management account or to service-linked roles ([RCPs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/organizations/latest/userguide/orgs_manage_policies_rcps.html)). Organizations account tags are invisible to IAM: the global condition key list contains no key exposing account or OU tags, so the hierarchy has to be copied onto workload roles at provisioning time or mirrored in the OU tree and matched with `aws:PrincipalOrgPaths` ([global condition keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_condition-keys.html)). Where accounts map one-to-one to a Product or Service, `aws:PrincipalAccount` is the one ownership attribute teams cannot forge, and the design should lean on it for the upper levels.

## Private VPCs need a second endpoint and cheap, per-GB math

A workload in a private VPC reaches EventBridge through an interface endpoint, and the two bus types use different ones: `com.amazonaws.<region>.events` serves Classic only, while the new bus requires a separate `com.amazonaws.<region>.eventsv2` endpoint ([EventBridge VPC endpoints](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-related-service-vpc.html)). IAM actions stay in the `events:` namespace for both; a policy naming `eventsv2:PutEvents` is accepted by IAM but "grants nothing" ([Names and endpoints](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-names.html)). Publishing from a spoke VPC to a bus in another account works because the bus ARN in the request, not the endpoint, selects the receiving account. AWS documents each part (ARN accepted by `PutEvents`, resource policy allowing other accounts, endpoint as a private path) but never states the combination in one sentence; it is a widely used pattern that still deserves a smoke test ([PutEventsRequestEntry](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/APIReference/API_PutEventsRequestEntry.html), [bus permissions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-event-bus-perms.html)).

The endpoint policy is a third independent gate alongside the identity policy and the bus policy, limited to 20,480 characters ([PrivateLink endpoint policies](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/vpc/latest/privatelink/vpc-endpoints-access.html)). Scoping it to the central bus ARN and `aws:PrincipalOrgID` blocks exfiltration to buses outside the organization. Whether the `eventsv2` endpoint supports endpoint policies was not confirmed, and unsupported services silently get full access, so this needs checking against the PrivateLink support table. Network-perimeter conditions on the bus itself are weaker ground: EventBridge's condition-key documentation lists no VPC keys, and the scalable `aws:VpceOrgID` key is documented for EventBridge Scheduler but not for `events`, with AWS warning that unsupported use "can lead to unintended authorization results" ([global condition keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/reference_policies_condition-keys.html)). Identity conditions should carry the bus policy; VPC conditions are an optional extra to add only after a test.

Delivery to consumers never enters the customer VPC. EventBridge hands events to SQS, Lambda or another service over the AWS network, and the private workload then reads through that service's own interface endpoint ([EventBridge VPC endpoints](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-related-service-vpc.html)). On the new bus the subscriber's target and delivery role must both belong to the subscriber owner's account, which lines up exactly with a spoke-owned module ([subscribers](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-subscribers.html)). The only route by which EventBridge pushes HTTP into a private VPC is an API destination bound to a VPC Lattice resource configuration, same Region only ([private APIs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/connection-private.html)).

On endpoint placement, AWS's multi-VPC networking whitepaper presents both models. An endpoint per VPC allows least-privilege policies per spoke; centralizing endpoints in a shared VPC behind Transit Gateway cuts endpoint count but forces one policy for everyone, a "larger blast radius", and manual private hosted zones ([AWS whitepaper](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/building-scalable-secure-multi-vpc-network-infrastructure/centralized-access-to-vpc-private-endpoints.html)). At the published example rate of $0.01 per endpoint per AZ per hour, one endpoint costs about $7.30 per AZ per month, so roughly $15 to $22 per VPC, doubled during any period when both `events` and `eventsv2` are needed ([PrivateLink pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/privatelink/pricing/)). For a design whose access model depends on per-spoke control, per-VPC endpoints are the better default, with centralization worth it only where a Transit Gateway already exists.

Cost attribution changes with the new bus. Classic charges $1.00 per million custom events and another $1.00 per million for each cross-account or bus-to-bus hop, billed to the sender ([pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/pricing/), [EventBridge User Guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-cross-account.html)). The new bus charges $0.18 per GB ingress for the first 5,000 GB, $0.05 per GB egress per delivery, and $0.08 per GB for storage beyond default retention, with events rounded up to 1 KB; "publishers pay for events ingested, and subscribers pay for events delivered" ([pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/pricing/), [AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-enhanced-custom-event-buses-in-amazon-eventbridge-for-enterprise-scale-event-driven-applications/)). By simple arithmetic, a million 1 KB events is about 1 GB, or roughly $0.18 against $1.00 on Classic, and the break-even on ingestion alone sits near 5.6 KB per event; large payloads cost more on the new bus than on Classic. These rates came from a summarized read of the pricing page and need confirming against the live page. Payload limits rose to 1 MB in January 2026 ([AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/01/amazon-eventbridge-increases-event-payload-size-256-kb-1-mb)). One operational quota deserves attention in Terraform: only **one subscriber create or delete can be in progress per bus across all accounts**, with a second call failing with `ConcurrentModificationException`, so parallel spoke applies will collide and need retries ([quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-quota.html)).

## Taxonomy and governance sit above the bus, in the modules

AWS publishes no normative naming standard. Its examples use dotted or reverse-DNS `source` values and noun-plus-past-tense `detail-type` values such as `OrderCreated`, with the advice that "names are typically difficult to change, so it's worthwhile thinking about setting a standard up-front" ([AWS Builder Center](https://builder.aws.com/content/2dhVUFPH16jZbhZfUB73aRVJ5uD/eventbridge-schema-registry-best-practices)). The same AWS-authored guidance recommends a metadata object carrying an event id, sequence id, tenant and data classification, and practitioners converge on a `detail.metadata` and `detail.data` envelope with id, version, domain, service and correlation id ([theburningmonk.com](https://theburningmonk.com/2024/11/eventbridge-best-practice-why-you-should-wrap-events-in-event-envelopes/)). On the new bus, the `Metadata` map of `PutRawEvents` is the natural home for the fields that double as authorization attributes. The practical rule is one vocabulary across three planes: the same hierarchy keys as event metadata keys, as role tags for `aws:PrincipalTag`, and as account tags for cost allocation, with allowed values governed by a single tag policy. Organizations account tags can now be activated for cost allocation, which gives chargeback of ingress and egress by hierarchy level without tagging resources ([AWS Billing User Guide](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/awsaccountbilling/latest/aboutv2/account-tags-cost-allocation.html)).

Schema governance is not something EventBridge supplies. The Schema Registry stores OpenAPI 3 and JSON Schema Draft 4 documents and can infer schemas, but discovery is unsupported on buses encrypted with a customer managed key, and the Amazon Key team notes that the registry "doesn't provide native validation" ([EventBridge schemas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-schema.html), [KMS encryption](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-encryption-event-bus-cmkey.html), [AWS Architecture Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/mastering-millisecond-latency-and-millions-of-events-the-event-driven-architecture-behind-the-amazon-key-suite)). AWS's organizational guidance assigns schema ownership and change management to producers and recommends decentralized ownership under standards set centrally ([AWS Architecture Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/best-practices-for-implementing-event-driven-architectures-in-your-organization)). Practitioner consensus on versioning is add-only evolution with the version in metadata, not in the event name ([theburningmonk.com](https://theburningmonk.com/2025/04/event-versioning-strategies-for-event-driven-architectures/)).

The case studies agree on one point that bears directly on the module design: none hands raw bus access to teams. KnowBe4 runs a single core bus across accounts with publisher and subscriber IaC modules that register events, record subscriptions in DynamoDB, and route publishes through an API that validates JSON Schema and stamps the `source` prefix from the caller's identity instead of trusting the publisher ([AWS Cloud Operations Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/mt/event-driven-architecture-using-amazon-eventbridge)). Amazon Key uses the single-bus, multi-account pattern with a custom schema repository, a generated client library and a subscriber constructs library ([AWS Architecture Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/mastering-millisecond-latency-and-millions-of-events-the-event-driven-architecture-behind-the-amazon-key-suite)). PostNL wraps EventBridge in a broker where producers register schemas and consumers self-subscribe through a governed interface ([lucvandonkersgoed.com](https://lucvandonkersgoed.com/2023/11/10/serverless-opentelemetry-at-scale-the-postnl-context/)). EventBridge has no subscription approval step; the nearest AWS analogue is Amazon DataZone, where the publishing project approves each request by default ([Amazon DataZone](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/datazone/latest/userguide/approve-reject-subscription-request.html)). In a Terraform design the equivalent control is pull-request review on the owner's allow-list input, with the owning team as required reviewer. All of these deployments predate the new bus, and no production migration to it has been published.

## Recommendation: build on the new bus, gated by a two-week proof

### Module shape

The central-bus module should own one Custom Event Bus per Region in a dedicated platform account (not the management account, where RCPs do not apply), a RAM share to the organization or selected OUs using the `PublishOnly` managed permission, and a generated `default` policy. That policy carries the constant-size publish guardrails binding each hierarchy metadata key to the matching principal tag, the own-subtree subscribe statement, the per-owner allow-list statements, and an `aws:PrincipalOrgID` condition on every Allow, since a wildcard principal without a caller-identifying condition is rejected with `PublicPolicyException` ([sharing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-sharing.html)). The hierarchy should be a list variable of level names that drives the metadata keys, the tag keys and the generated conditions together, so changing Platform -> Solution -> Product -> Service to another shape is a variable change. The module also owns retention (AWS suggests 7 days as a practical default), per-consumer `EventsDelivered` and `EgressBytes` alarms on the `SubscriberAccount` dimension, and a documented `RevokeResource` runbook ([AWS Compute Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/compute/building-event-driven-applications-at-scale-with-amazon-eventbridge/), [sharing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-sharing.html)). Direct publishers should be required to use `PutRawEvents`, because metadata, and therefore the whole ABAC model, exists only there.

The spoke-connection module should own the `eventsv2` interface endpoint with `private_dns_enabled = true` (Terraform defaults it to false) and an endpoint policy, the workload role with protected hierarchy tags and an identity policy for the central bus ARN (both sides must allow), and optional subscribers each with a metadata filter, an SQS or Lambda target in the spoke account, a delivery role and a dead-letter queue ([aws_vpc_endpoint docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hashicorp/terraform-provider-aws/main/website/docs/r/vpc_endpoint.html.markdown), [subscribers](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-subscribers.html)). Its `allowed_consumers` input, set by the owning service, feeds the central module's allow-list statements. Organization-level SCPs protecting the hierarchy tag keys belong in a separate guardrail stack that must exist before any spoke is onboarded.

### Provider support

The Classic resources (`aws_cloudwatch_event_bus`, `aws_cloudwatch_event_bus_policy`, `aws_cloudwatch_event_rule`, `aws_cloudwatch_event_target`) are complete in `hashicorp/aws`, but the changelog through 6.73.0 (11 November 2026) contains no `eventsv2` resource, and a third-party post confirms the absence in 6.69.1 ([changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hashicorp/terraform-provider-aws/main/CHANGELOG.md), [issoh.co.jp](https://www.issoh.co.jp/tech/details/18447/)). CloudFormation supports the new bus under the `AWS::EventsV2` namespace, so the interim route is the `awscc` provider or `aws_cloudformation_stack`, combined with the existing RAM and VPC endpoint resources ([Names and endpoints](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-names.html)). The exact `awscc` resource names were not verified. Isolating the bus, policy and subscriber resources behind module interfaces keeps the later swap to native resources contained. If a Classic fallback is ever needed, use `aws_cloudwatch_event_bus_policy`, never `aws_cloudwatch_event_permission`: the two are mutually incompatible and the latter can only express `aws:PrincipalOrgID` ([provider docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hashicorp/terraform-provider-aws/main/website/docs/r/cloudwatch_event_bus_policy.html.markdown)). The community module `terraform-aws-modules/eventbridge` inherits that limit through its `permissions` map and shows no new-bus support ([module README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/terraform-aws-modules/terraform-aws-eventbridge/master/README.md)).

### Open risks to close before committing

The proof of concept should settle five things, in order of how much design they invalidate if they fail. First, whether `events:Metadata/<key>` conditions accept `${aws:PrincipalTag/...}` on `PutRawEvents` and on subscriber actions, in both identity and bus policies, across accounts; if not, the fallback is account-based ownership via `aws:PrincipalAccount` and literal statements, or a publish API that stamps metadata from caller identity as KnowBe4 does. Second, whether the `default` policy size and its adjustable ceiling fit the expected number of owner allow-lists. Third, whether the `eventsv2` endpoint supports endpoint policies. Fourth, whether the bus is available in the target Region and how forwarded AWS-service events behave, given that a forwarder carrying both AWS and custom events needs both `PutEvents` and `PutRawEvents` or "granting only one denies half the stream" ([sharing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/eventbridge/latest/userguide/eb-custom-bus-sharing.html)). Fifth, live pricing and the default free retention period, which the research did not capture. Smaller unknowns remain: limits on metadata keys and values, whether RAM customer managed permissions accept metadata conditions with variables, and the CloudFormation-backed Terraform experience under the one-at-a-time subscriber quota.

The steelman for staying on Classic is real: it has native Terraform support, six years of documented patterns, and availability in every Region. But on Classic the hierarchy collapses into a naming convention on `source`, subscribe-side control rests on undocumented projection of pattern operators into condition keys, and the central team ends up owning rules for every consumer. Those are structural limits, whereas the new bus's gaps are tooling maturity and a few tests.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the question. "ABAC on EventBridge" turns out to be two different problems with different answers: ownership of what is published is a true attribute match and fits a constant-size policy, while cross-team read access is an entitlement list that only looks like ABAC. Designing them as separate mechanisms, with the allow-list treated as data owned by the producing service and reviewed like code, avoids forcing IAM to express something it handles poorly and gives the platform the subscription registry that every mature deployment ended up building anyway.

The second shift is where the risk sits. AWS has documented the new bus's authorization semantics in unusual detail, including its traps, yet has published nothing on tag-variable policies for it and no customer has reported a production migration. The design is therefore sound on paper and unverified in practice, and its cheapest insurance is architectural: keep hierarchy keys, policy generation and bus resources behind module boundaries, so that a failed test or a late-arriving native Terraform resource changes one module's internals and nothing else.
